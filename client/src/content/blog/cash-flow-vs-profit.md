# Cash Flow vs Profit: The Difference That Saves Businesses

**You can be profitable and still go bankrupt.** This isn't a paradox—it's one of the most misunderstood realities in business finance.

## The Core Distinction

### Profit (Accounting Reality)
**Profit = Revenue - Expenses** on your income statement (P&L).

It's an *accounting concept* that follows accrual rules:
- Revenue is recognized when *earned* (not when cash is received)
- Expenses are recognized when *incurred* (not when cash is paid)

### Cash Flow (Operational Reality)
**Cash Flow = Cash In - Cash Out** in your bank account.

It's the *actual money* moving through your business:
- Cash received from customers
- Cash paid to vendors, employees, and creditors
- Cash used for equipment, inventory, and debt payments

## Why Profitable Businesses Fail

### Example: The Growing Contractor

**Month 1:**
- Lands $100,000 contract
- Books $100,000 revenue (profit looks great!)
- Receives $0 cash (payment terms: Net 60)
- Pays $60,000 for labor and materials (cash out)

**Result:**
- P&L shows $40,000 profit
- Bank account is -$60,000
- Can't make payroll next month

This is called **profitable but cash-starved**—and it kills businesses.

## The 5 Cash Flow Killers

### 1. Slow-Paying Customers
**The Problem:** You invoice $50,000 but don't get paid for 60-90 days.

**The Fix:**
- Require deposits (30-50% upfront)
- Offer early payment discounts (2% if paid in 10 days)
- Invoice immediately upon completion
- Follow up on overdue invoices weekly

### 2. Inventory Buildup
**The Problem:** You buy $100,000 in inventory that sits unsold for 6 months.

**The Fix:**
- Implement just-in-time inventory
- Negotiate consignment terms with suppliers
- Track inventory turnover ratio (should be 4-6x annually for most businesses)
- Discount slow-moving inventory aggressively

### 3. Rapid Growth
**The Problem:** More sales = more expenses *before* you collect revenue.

**The Fix:**
- Secure a line of credit before you need it
- Raise prices to slow growth to sustainable levels
- Require larger deposits on new projects
- Stagger project start dates to manage cash needs

### 4. Fixed Asset Purchases
**The Problem:** Buying a $50,000 vehicle shows as depreciation expense ($10,000/year) but costs $50,000 cash immediately.

**The Fix:**
- Lease instead of buy when possible
- Finance equipment to spread payments
- Use Section 179 deduction strategically
- Time purchases around cash flow peaks

### 5. Debt Payments
**The Problem:** Principal payments on loans don't show on P&L but drain cash.

**The Fix:**
- Refinance high-interest debt
- Negotiate longer payment terms
- Use cash flow forecasting to plan payments
- Consider debt consolidation

## The Cash Flow Statement: Your Early Warning System

Your cash flow statement has three sections:

### Operating Activities (The Core)
Cash from day-to-day business operations:
- Collections from customers
- Payments to suppliers
- Payroll and operating expenses

**Healthy businesses generate positive operating cash flow.**

### Investing Activities
Cash used for long-term assets:
- Equipment purchases
- Real estate
- Business acquisitions

**Negative is normal for growing businesses.**

### Financing Activities
Cash from/to investors and lenders:
- Loan proceeds
- Loan repayments
- Owner distributions

**Should be positive in early stages, negative as you mature.**

## The 13-Week Cash Flow Forecast

The most powerful tool for avoiding cash crises is a **rolling 13-week cash flow forecast**.

### How It Works:

**Week 1:**
- Beginning cash: $50,000
- Expected collections: $30,000
- Expected payments: $40,000
- Ending cash: $40,000

**Week 2:**
- Beginning cash: $40,000
- Expected collections: $20,000
- Expected payments: $35,000
- Ending cash: $25,000

**Week 3:**
- Beginning cash: $25,000
- Expected collections: $15,000
- Expected payments: $45,000
- **Ending cash: -$5,000** ⚠️ **PROBLEM IDENTIFIED**

### Action Steps When You See a Problem:
1. Accelerate collections (call customers, offer discounts)
2. Delay non-critical payments
3. Draw on line of credit
4. Negotiate payment plans with vendors
5. Reduce discretionary spending

## Key Metrics to Track Weekly

### 1. Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)
**Formula:** (Accounts Receivable ÷ Revenue) × 365

**Target:** 30-45 days for most businesses

**What it means:** How long it takes to collect payment after a sale.

### 2. Days Payable Outstanding (DPO)
**Formula:** (Accounts Payable ÷ Cost of Goods Sold) × 365

**Target:** Match or exceed DSO

**What it means:** How long you take to pay suppliers.

### 3. Cash Conversion Cycle
**Formula:** DSO + Days Inventory Outstanding - DPO

**Target:** As low as possible (negative is ideal)

**What it means:** How long cash is tied up in operations.

### 4. Operating Cash Flow Ratio
**Formula:** Operating Cash Flow ÷ Current Liabilities

**Target:** > 1.0

**What it means:** Can you cover short-term obligations with operating cash?

## Real Example: E-commerce Business

**Scenario:**
- Monthly revenue: $200,000
- Monthly expenses: $150,000
- Monthly profit: $50,000
- **But cash flow is negative. Why?**

**The Reality:**
- Inventory purchased 60 days before sale: $120,000 cash out
- Customer payments via credit card: 3-day delay
- Supplier payments: Due immediately
- **Cash gap: 63 days**

**The Fix:**
- Negotiate Net 30 terms with suppliers (saves $120,000 immediate cash)
- Require 50% deposit on custom orders
- Use inventory financing for seasonal buildup
- Maintain $100,000 line of credit for buffer

**Result:**
- Same profit
- Positive cash flow
- Business survives and thrives

## Warning Signs of Cash Flow Problems

🚨 **Immediate Action Required:**
- Payroll is tight
- Using credit cards for operating expenses
- Delaying vendor payments
- Skipping estimated tax payments
- Owner not taking salary

⚠️ **Address Soon:**
- DSO increasing
- Inventory turnover slowing
- Operating cash flow declining
- Line of credit usage increasing
- Customer concentration risk (>25% from one customer)

## The Bottom Line

**Profit is an opinion. Cash flow is a fact.**

You can manipulate profit with accounting choices. You can't manipulate cash—it's either in your account or it's not.

**Profitable businesses with poor cash flow fail. Breakeven businesses with strong cash flow survive.**

Master cash flow, and you master your business.
